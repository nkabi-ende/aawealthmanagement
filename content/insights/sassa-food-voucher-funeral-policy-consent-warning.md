Insurance should protect a household from financial shocks. It should never become the shock itself. Yet reports in 2025 and 2026 describe social-grant beneficiaries—often older people—who believed they were being registered for food parcels, vouchers or other assistance, and later discovered funeral-policy premiums being deducted from their grants.

The concern is not whether funeral cover has value. It can. The concern is whether a person genuinely understood **what they were agreeing to, who would receive their information, what the policy would cost and how the premium would be collected**.

## What has been reported

In June 2026, [Sowetan reported accounts of elderly beneficiaries](https://www.sowetan.co.za/news/2026-06-08-watch-elderly-duped-into-funeral-cover-with-offer-of-free-food-parcels/) saying they were promised food parcels and subsequently found funeral cover linked to their grants. The [Witness also reported complaints in KwaZulu-Natal](https://witness.co.za/news/2026/06/25/unauthorised-deductions-to-pensioners-sassa-grants/) involving voucher pitches and disputed deductions. More recently, [GroundUp reported on a pensioner's refunded deduction](https://groundup.org.za/article/insurer-refunds-khayelitsha-pensioner-after-we-query-funeral-policy-deductions/), illustrating why a clear route to dispute and redress matters.

These are reported allegations and documented disputes, not proof that every policy or grant deduction is unauthorised. The facts must be investigated case by case, with insurers and intermediaries given an opportunity to respond.

SASSA has [publicly warned beneficiaries](https://www.gov.za/news/media-statements/sassa-stops-illegal-deductions-social-grants-03-sep-2025) that it does not sell funeral cover or loans. It says a legally permitted funeral-policy deduction depends on the beneficiary's consent and is subject to limits under the social-assistance rules.

## The dangerous promise: a voucher in exchange for personal details

A food voucher sounds like immediate relief, especially to someone living on a fixed grant. That makes the pitch unusually powerful. The reported pattern is straightforward:

- Someone approaches a pensioner or disability-grant recipient near a payment point, community venue, shop or home.
- The conversation begins with a supposed food parcel, voucher, competition entry or other benefit—not with an explanation of a recurring insurance premium.
- The person is asked for identifying information, a signature or another form of verification.
- Later, an insurance policy or monthly deduction appears that the beneficiary says they did not knowingly authorise.

**Providing an ID number or accepting food is not the same thing as knowingly consenting to insurance.** A responsible sale must make the actual product, price, insurer, intermediary and consequences clear before an application is processed.

## What about claims involving SASSA or political organisations?

One of the warning signs is a salesperson implying that an offer has government, welfare-programme or political-party backing. Community allegations have also referred to names such as the MK Party in connection with voucher promises. **We have not independently verified that the MK Party authorised, organised or participated in the conduct described here, and the use of any organisation's name is not proof of its involvement.**

The proper response is to verify the specific salesperson, FSP, insurer and any claimed affiliation independently. An official-looking logo, a party name or a claimed grant benefit should never be treated as a substitute for informed consent.

## Why this hurts the entire insurance industry

The immediate harm is felt by the household. Money removed without meaningful permission is money not available for food, electricity, transport or medicine. The wider damage is serious too.

- **Trust erodes.** Families become suspicious of legitimate insurance advisers and the value of funeral cover itself.
- **Good intermediaries are undermined.** Brokers who invest in proper disclosures, suitability and after-sales service compete against misleading pitches.
- **Insurers carry avoidable risk.** Disputed mandates create complaints, investigations, cancellations, refunds and reputational damage.
- **Vulnerable customers withdraw.** Fear can prevent people from seeking insurance that may genuinely suit their needs.

For a sector that exists to manage risk, clear consent must be a measurable operating standard—not a formality at the end of a sales script.

## What beneficiaries and families should do

1. **Ask the real question.** Is this a free benefit, an insurance application or both? If insurance is involved, ask for the insurer's name, FSP details, monthly premium and full written terms.
2. **Do not hand over sensitive details casually.** Never provide ID copies, fingerprints or banking credentials merely because someone offers a parcel or voucher. Use the insurer's verified channels when documentation is genuinely necessary.
3. **Review your grant and bank deductions.** If something unfamiliar appears, keep statements, policy messages and the salesperson's details.
4. **Challenge a disputed policy promptly.** Ask the insurer or intermediary for the application, full consent record and cancellation/refund process. Escalate to SASSA where grant deductions are involved.
5. **Use official complaint channels.** Contact SASSA via [sassa.gov.za](https://www.sassa.gov.za/) or its published toll-free line **0800 60 10 11**. You can also seek guidance through the appropriate financial-sector ombud or regulator.
6. **Help an older relative read before signing.** Explain the ongoing cost, beneficiaries, waiting periods, exclusions and cancellation terms in language they understand.

A genuine, voluntarily chosen funeral policy should not be dismissed merely because it is paid from a grant. The issue is whether the sale and mandate were lawful, transparent and understood.

## Six changes brokers, insurers and sales teams should make now

**1. Separate assistance from sales.** Never make a welfare benefit, food parcel or political promise the gateway to an undisclosed policy application.

**2. Prove informed consent.** Record a clear explanation of the product and premium, and retain evidence that the customer understood and actively chose the policy and deduction method.

**3. Monitor high-risk distribution channels.** Audit sales at grant pay points and community campaigns. Check who recruited the agent, what scripts were used and which third parties received data.

**4. Verify beyond a checkbox.** Where required and lawful, use independent confirmation in the customer's language, targeted post-sale verification and effective fraud flags before deductions begin.

**5. Reward quality, not merely volume.** Monitor complaints, early cancellations, refund rates, disputed mandates and persistent agent-level anomalies alongside sales totals.

**6. Make redress simple.** Offer a visible support contact, a documented complaint reference, clear escalation timelines and a workable refund review process.

These controls should complement, not replace, the FAIS obligations and insurer requirements applicable to each business.
